**PILLAR** · OPEN · **SECTION** · EDITOR'S LETTER

**HEADLINE** · The women
**who raised us.**

**STANDFIRST** · Four men put this magazine together every month. Not one of us got here on his own.

---

There is a sound every South African knows.

Your full name, all of it, in order, called from another room. No child has ever needed that explained. Three syllables and you stopped whatever you were doing.

The women who could do that to us fed people who arrived unannounced. They stretched money that had no business stretching and never once mentioned it. They worked when they were sick and sat up when we were.

Between the four of us that is a lot of years, and we have spent most of them not saying thank you properly.

So this month we are saying it.

August belongs to the women of this country, and the risk with a month like that is it becomes something we mention rather than something we mean. This edition tries to mean it. You will meet women who build things, feed people and refuse to give up on places everyone else has written off. Our cover story goes back to a Thursday in 1956, and to twenty thousand women who walked to the Union Buildings and then stood in complete silence for half an hour. Read that one slowly.

Now the part where you come in.

Page 24 has ten questions on it for your mother or your grandmother. Screenshot it, or just hold your phone between the two of you and read them out loud. Either way, ask them properly and listen to the whole answer.

Then send us what she said. Post the funniest one on our Facebook page, because the funny ones are always the best ones, and whichever answer makes the most of us laugh goes into September.

More of you write to us every month now. You correct us when we get it wrong, you send us people we would never have found, and the magazine is better for it. Keep going.

One more thing. When you finish reading this, phone her.

Have the Moxie.

## PAGE 06 · WHY A THURSDAY

**DEVICE:** Standard cream editorial page. One pull quote, Playfair Italic 16pt orange, 3pt teal left rule, sitting two thirds down.

Somebody had to choose the day, and the choice tells you how carefully this was planned.

Thursday was the day domestic workers had off. Across the country, thousands of women who cleaned other people's houses and raised other people's children had one afternoon that belonged to them, and it fell on a Thursday. Pick any other day and most of them cannot come.

So Thursday it was. 9 August 1956.

The organisation behind it was the Federation of South African Women, formed two years earlier to give women a voice inside a movement mostly run by men.

> **PULL QUOTE** · A woman's place is not in the kitchen. A woman's place is everywhere.

What they were protesting was a proposed change to the law requiring black women to carry a passbook.

For a reader who has never seen one, a passbook was a small booklet you had to carry at all times. It recorded who you were, where you were allowed to be and who you worked for. A policeman could stop you and demand it, and if you did not have it, you could be arrested on the spot. Black men had carried them for years. The plan was to extend the system to women.

The women of FEDSAW understood exactly what that meant in practice, and they wrote it down plainly. That homes would be broken up when mothers were arrested. That women and girls would be searched by policemen in the street. That a woman would lose the right to move freely from one place to another.

They decided to say so, in person, at the front door of the government.

Here is the detail most people do not know, and it turns this from a march into something cleverer.

Large gatherings were banned. The authorities knew something was coming and had made it illegal to assemble in a group.

So the women did not assemble.

They arrived in Pretoria by train and bus and taxi and on foot, from Johannesburg and Cape Town and Durban and villages nobody outside the district had heard of. And then they walked up towards the Union Buildings in twos and threes.

### **2 · 3**
*Two women walking together is not a gathering.*

It is two women walking. Three women with shopping bags going up a hill on a Thursday afternoon is nothing at all, and there is no law against it.

Thousands of pairs. Thousands of small, unremarkable, entirely legal groups, all moving in the same direction at the same time towards the same place.

They converged on the gardens and the amphitheatre. By the time anybody realised what was happening, twenty thousand women were standing on the terraces below the Union Buildings, and there was nothing to be done about it, because not one of them had broken a single rule to get there.

Many wore traditional dress. Many wore the green and black and gold of the Congress. A great number had babies tied to their backs, because they had come a long way and there was nobody at home to leave them with.

## PAGE 10 · THIRTY MINUTES

**DEVICE:** Top half full-bleed charcoal with the numeral **30** at 200pt Barlow Condensed Bold in burnt orange, and MINUTES beneath at 24pt white, wide letter-spacing. Body copy on cream below. Strongest page in the spread.

### **30**
**MINUTES**

They came back down to the amphitheatre, and then they did the thing nobody expected.

They stood still, and they stopped talking.

Twenty thousand people, in the open air, in complete silence, for half an hour.

Try it. Not for thirty minutes. Two, in a room with your family. Two minutes of deliberate silence with other people is close to unbearable. Somebody always laughs, or coughs, or has to say something. Two minutes.

They did thirty. Outside. In front of the building that had just declined to receive them. With babies on their backs.

There were no microphones and no screens and no way to coordinate anything. It simply held, and held, and held.

And then, when it ended, they sang. The line they sang that day has outlived every person who was there and every law they were protesting. You strike a woman, you strike a rock.

Then twenty thousand women walked back down the hill, in twos and threes, and went home to make supper.

# PAGE 13 · 5 THINGS YOU DIDN'T KNOW

**PILLAR** · DISCOVER · **SECTION** · 5 THINGS YOU DIDN'T KNOW

**KICKER** · SOUTH AFRICAN WOMEN

**HEADLINE** · Five women you have
**never heard enough about.**

**STANDFIRST** · One of them is on a box in your kitchen cupboard right now.

---

### 01 · OUMA IS A REAL PERSON

Molteno in the Eastern Cape is the coldest town in South Africa, and in 1939 the Depression had flattened it. The dominee handed each woman in his congregation half a crown and told them to go and multiply it. Elizabeth Ann Greyvensteyn, known as Ouma Nannie, bought flour and baked her family's buttermilk rusks. They sold out at the church bazaar in minutes. In 1941 she took the very first loan the Industrial Development Corporation ever granted. The factory is still in Molteno, still on the same farm, and it employs more than 250 people.

### 02 · THE GALLOPING GRANNY

Mavis Hutchison of Kimberley was a sickly, painfully shy child whose twin sister did the talking for her. She started running at 36. At 53, in 1978, she ran from Los Angeles City Hall to New York City Hall, roughly 5 000 kilometres, averaging 72 kilometres a day for 69 days, and became the first woman in the world to run across America. On day 33 shin splints reduced her to walking, and she carried on anyway. At 86 she was still winning gold at the SA Masters, in the sprints and the javelin.

### 03 · SHE HAD TO LEAVE THE CONTINENT TO GET A DEGREE

Charlotte Mannya Maxeke joined a touring choir to America in the 1890s. The tour collapsed and stranded her there, so she stayed and studied instead, at Wilberforce University in Ohio, where one of her lecturers was W.E.B. Du Bois. She came home with a BSc and became the first black South African woman to hold a university degree. No institution in the country of her birth would have admitted her.

### 04 · THE FIRST BLACK WOMAN DOCTOR NEARLY NEVER EXISTED

Mary Malahlela's parents fled their village before she was old enough to remember it, because they refused to put her newborn twin brothers to death as custom demanded. South African medical schools only began admitting black students in 1941. She was in the first intake, on the first scholarship of its kind ever awarded, and qualified in 1947 as the first black woman doctor in the country. She practised for 34 years and died at work, seeing a patient at a rural clinic where she volunteered.

### 05 · BOTH SIDES OF EVEREST

Cathy O'Dowd grew up in Johannesburg describing herself as unsporty and academic, and only started climbing at university. She answered a newspaper advert looking for a South African woman for an Everest expedition, and six months later stood on the summit. In 1999 she went up the other side, becoming the first woman in the world to have climbed Everest from both north and south. She remains the only one.

# PAGES 14-15 · THE QUIET HERO

Somebody sits at a kitchen table and writes a letter to a child they will never meet.

Often that somebody is nine years old.

They do not know the name of the child who will read it. They do not know the age, beyond baby, child or teen. They do not know where that child will sleep tonight, or next week, or next year. They write anyway. Something short, usually. You are brave. You matter. Somebody is thinking about you.

Then the note goes into a backpack alongside a toothbrush and pyjamas and a soft toy with a ribbon round its neck, and the bag goes to KidsCare in Olympus, Pretoria East.

Weeks later, a child holds it.

## For kids, from kids

That line is on everything KidsCare produces, and it is not decoration. It is the design.

Chanel Long built the organisation around the idea that children should be the ones doing the packing. Not adults organising a drive on behalf of children. Children choosing the toy, checking the sizes, writing the note in their own handwriting, with all the spelling exactly as it comes.

Which produces something an adult cannot produce. A child receiving that bag is not being helped from above by an institution. They are being thought about by somebody their own age who has never met them and did it anyway.

KidsCare puts its values in four words: kindness, compassion, community, care. The unusual part is that the project teaches all four to the children doing the giving at the same time as it delivers them to the children receiving.

## What arrives with nothing

There is a moment nobody photographs. A child leaves a home that has stopped being safe and travels to a place of safety, and the whole thing takes an hour or two, and there is no time to pack.

Most arrive with what they are wearing. That is the entire inventory.

The practical gap is obvious. No clothes, no toothbrush, and for a baby no nappies and no bottle. Somebody has to solve that tonight.

But the practical half is the smaller half. The other half is that a child has just lost everything at once, including any evidence that the world contains people who want good things for them.

A backpack answers both.

## Why it has to be a bag

KidsCare gives the bags to registered organisations, who hand them to children as they enter a place of safety. From that point, the bag travels.

Children in care move. Place of safety, then foster care, then perhaps a family member, then somewhere else again. Each move happens fast and each one is decided by adults. The backpack goes along every time.

For many children it becomes the only constant thing they own. Not a bed, not a room, not a school. A bag. Everything inside it was theirs before they arrived and will still be theirs when they leave.

Which is why nothing in it is second-hand. KidsCare will not take used items, and that rule is not about hygiene. A child who has spent their life receiving other people's leftovers can tell a gift from a cast-off in about four seconds.

## How your family builds one

Simpler than most people expect.

Collect a free backpack, a packing list and a label from Olympus in Pretoria East. Pick an age group. Then go shopping, and take your children with you, because choosing is the part that teaches.

The packing list is specific and it matters. Correct clothing size, correct underwear size, correct nappy size, age-appropriate bottle or sippy cup, age-appropriate formula. Extras are welcome and people add them, but the basics have to be right. A bag with the wrong size nappies helps nobody.

Then the note gets written. Then you hand it in.

If shopping is not practical, KidsCare will build it for you. R850 covers a Build-A-Bag or a Dignity Bag, R400 a Brave Heart Bag, R250 a Trauma Bag. Backpacks themselves are the organisation's biggest single expense, so contributions towards the winter and Christmas backpack drives stretch further than almost anything else.

And this scales beautifully. A class. A grade. A youth group. A staff team. Ten children around a table with ten backpacks and ten packing lists is one afternoon, and at the end of it ten children somewhere else own something.

## The unglamorous part

This work runs on readiness rather than response.

A recent KidsCare appeal showed how fast South Africans move once they understand a need, and that generosity is not in question. The problem is timing. No child is removed from a home on a schedule that suits a fundraising campaign. The bag has to already exist, packed and waiting on a shelf, on a day nobody saw coming.

Which makes an ordinary Tuesday the useful day to do this. Not a crisis, not an appeal. A Tuesday, a shopping list, a backpack, and ten minutes for a child in your house to write something kind to a child they will never meet.

Small acts. Kind hearts. That is the whole thing.

## PAGE 19 · CHAPTER ONE

**SECTION LABEL** · STILL UNSOLVED · FLIGHTS TO NOWHERE

**KICKER** · BY WOUTER BOTES

**HEADLINE** · The 727
**that vanished**

**STANDFIRST** · An airliner is not an easy thing to hide. Twenty-three years later, nobody has found this one.

---

A Boeing 727 is not easy to hide. The sheer size of the aircraft would stand out anywhere, particularly in a place where large aeroplanes are rare. Changing the colour and paint scheme would help. So would a hangar big enough to take it. Either way, the job would be enormous.

Somebody managed it.

**LUANDA, 25 MAY 2003**

The aircraft was a Boeing 727-223, registered N844AA, built in 1975 and flown for twenty-five years by American Airlines. By 2003 it belonged to a Miami company, Aerospace Sales and Leasing. It had been sitting at Quatro de Fevereiro Airport in Luanda for fourteen months, grounded and idle, on lease to TAAG Angola Airlines and in the process of being converted for use by IRS Airlines.

It no longer looked like an airliner inside. The passenger seats had been stripped out, because the aircraft had been outfitted to carry diesel fuel. On the outside it was unpainted silver with a stripe of blue, white and red.

At around five that afternoon, two men walked out to it. One was Ben C. Padilla, an American pilot and flight engineer. The other was John M. Mutantu, a mechanic hired from the Democratic Republic of Congo.

Neither man was licensed or certified to fly a 727. The aircraft requires three aircrew for operational flight. Both had been working with Angolan mechanics in the preceding weeks to get it flight ready, and American investigators later assumed Padilla was at the controls. Airport workers could not even agree on whether one man boarded or two.

**NO RADIO, NO LIGHTS, NO CLEARANCE**

The 727 began taxiing without a single word to the control tower. It moved aimlessly for a while, then entered the runway. The tower tried to make contact and got nothing back.

Without any lights showing, it took off, turned southwest out over the Atlantic Ocean, and disappeared.

It carried 53 000 litres of fuel, giving it a range of roughly 2 400 kilometres.

**THEN THE SEARCH**

The FBI became involved, along with the US Department of State. American agents made enquiries across Africa and as far away as Sri Lanka. A reported sighting in Conakry, Guinea, was dismissed by US authorities. In 2010, Air and Space Magazine published an investigation and interviewed people close to the case. Nobody reached a conclusion.

Padilla's sister, Benita Padilla-Kirkland, told a South Florida newspaper in 2004 that she believed her brother had either crashed somewhere in Africa or was being held by some militant group.

**WHERE IS N844AA?**

Only a few scenarios make sense. Was the aircraft stolen to order and delivered to a buyer? If so, it would have had to be substantially altered, because it was far too recognisable to hide as it was. Did it crash instead, which is entirely plausible given that neither man aboard was certified on the type? The possible crash sites are guesswork, but the jungles near the equator and the Atlantic itself are the obvious candidates. Debris from an ocean crash usually washes up somewhere eventually. None ever has.

To this day nothing conclusive has been found. No aircraft, no crash site, no clue. Neither Padilla nor Mutantu has been seen or heard from again. It is as if a large airliner vanished into thin air. If the wreck lies in dense forest, it will only ever be found by chance, and that chance is remote almost to the point of impossibility.

Perhaps the Atlantic has one more secret to keep.

**HEADLINE** · One dough,
**four names, a hundred years.**

**STANDFIRST** · Vetkoek. Amagwinya. Magwinya. Fat cakes. The same warm thing in a paper bag, and more South African households have been kept afloat by it than anybody has ever counted.

---

Flour, water, salt, sugar, yeast. That is the entire list.

Nothing on it is expensive and nothing on it goes off, which is precisely why this recipe has survived everything the last hundred years has thrown at it. When money is short, a family can still eat. When money is very short, a family can still eat and sell the rest.

That second part is the story most people skip.

Walk past any school gate at half past two. Any taxi rank at six in the morning. Any roadside stand on a national road, any church fete, any factory shift change. Somebody is selling vetkoek out of a warm box, and the overwhelming likelihood is that somebody is a woman.

She got up at four. She mixed the dough in the dark, waited for it to prove, and fried it in a pot she has used for years. What comes back covers school fees, or a uniform, or the electricity, or the gap between what came in this month and what has to go out.

There are South African doctors and engineers and teachers whose entire education was paid for in twelve rand increments, one paper bag at a time, by a mother who never once described what she was doing as a business.

The name changes depending on which kitchen you grew up in. Vetkoek in Afrikaans. Amagwinya in isiZulu and isiXhosa. Magwinya in Sesotho and Setswana. Fat cakes in English, which is the least appealing name any food has ever been given.

Same dough. Same pot. Same hour of the morning.

What goes inside is where households genuinely disagree, and the argument is enjoyable and completely unresolvable. Curried mince is the standard. Apricot jam and a slab of cheddar has fierce support. Some people will only eat them plain with butter and syrup. There is a faction that puts polony in, and the rest of the country has opinions about that faction.

Make them on a cold Saturday. Let the children shape the dough, because it is forgiving and slightly ridiculous and nobody has ever made an ugly vetkoek.

Then eat them standing up in the kitchen, which is the correct way, whatever anybody tells you.

**447 words.**

## PAGE 21 · THE RECIPE CARD

# VETKOEK
### Makes 12 · Preparation 20 minutes · Proving 1 hour · Frying 20 minutes

### FOR THE DOUGH

- 500g cake flour
- 10g instant dry yeast, one sachet
- 15ml sugar
- 5ml salt
- 350ml lukewarm water
- Sunflower oil for frying, about 1 litre

### FOR THE CURRIED MINCE

- 30ml sunflower oil
- 1 onion, finely chopped
- 2 garlic cloves, crushed
- 15ml mild curry powder
- 5ml turmeric
- 500g beef mince
- 1 large tomato, grated, or 15ml tomato paste
- 15ml apricot jam
- 15ml brown vinegar
- 125ml water
- Salt and pepper

### METHOD

**1 · Make the dough.** Mix the flour, yeast, sugar and salt in a large bowl. Add the lukewarm water and mix to a soft, slightly sticky dough. Turn it onto a floured surface and knead for eight to ten minutes until it is smooth and springs back when you press it.

**2 · Prove.** Return it to the bowl, cover with a damp cloth and leave somewhere warm for about an hour, until it has doubled. On a cold August day, put the bowl on a chair next to a heater and give it a little longer.

**3 · Start the mince.** Heat the oil and fry the onion until soft. Add the garlic, curry powder and turmeric and stir for one minute so the spices wake up. Add the mince and brown it, breaking up the lumps.

**4 · Simmer.** Stir in the tomato, apricot jam, vinegar, water, salt and pepper. Cover and simmer gently for twenty minutes, adding a splash more water if it dries out. Taste and correct the salt.

**5 · Shape.** Knock the dough down and divide into twelve pieces. Roll each into a ball, flatten slightly, and leave on a floured tray for fifteen minutes.

**6 · Fry.** Heat the oil in a deep pot to about 170°C. If you have no thermometer, drop in a small piece of dough. It should rise and colour in roughly a minute. Fry three or four at a time, turning once, for four to five minutes until deep golden and hollow-sounding when tapped.

**7 · Drain and fill.** Rest on kitchen paper. Split each one open while still warm and spoon the mince in.

**THREE OTHER FILLINGS**
Apricot jam with a thick slice of cheddar · Butter and golden syrup · Grated biltong with cream cheese

**MOXIE TIP**
The oil temperature is the whole recipe. Too cool and they drink oil and go heavy. Too hot and they brown outside while the middle stays raw dough. Keep it steady at 170°C, fry in small batches, and let the oil come back up to heat between them. Children can do every single step of this recipe except this one.

## THE FULL NAME

Nothing else in this country carries the same weight. Not your first name. Your whole name, every part of it, in the correct order, called from another room.

You did not need to ask what it was about. You already knew. Your body knew before your brain caught up.

The remarkable thing is that it works in every language and every household in South Africa, and every single person reading this understood the previous paragraph immediately.

---

## THE CLASSICS

**"Ek gaan tot drie tel."**
*I am counting to three.*
Nobody has ever found out what happens at three. Not one South African alive has tested it.

**"Ek is nie 'n bank nie."**
*I am not a bank.*

**"Maak die deur toe, ons maak nie die hele buurt warm nie."**
*Close the door, we are not heating the whole neighbourhood.*

**"Uzobona."**
*You will see.*
Delivered quietly, which was always worse.

**"Is jou pa se van dan Kersvader?"**
*Is your father's surname Father Christmas?*

**"Ha ke na chelete."**
*I do not have money.*
Said with complete conviction, immediately before finding the money.

**"Wag tot jou pa hoor."**
*Wait until your father hears.*

**"Ndikuxelele."**
*I told you.*
Two syllables. Total victory.

**"As jou maats in 'n put spring, gaan jy ook?"**
*If your friends jump in a well, will you also?*

**"Ek het nie hierdie kos gemaak vir niks nie."**
*I did not make this food for nothing.*

**"Hayi bo."**
Not a sentence. Does not need to be.

**"Jy sal verstaan as jy 'n ma is."**
*You will understand when you are a mother.*
And the annoying part is that everybody eventually did.

---

## THE ONE THAT WAS NOT A THREAT

Every household also had one that has stayed for entirely different reasons. A thing she said when you got something wrong, or came home flattened, or could not see how the week was going to work.

Not funny. Not loud. Usually about eight words long, said once, and it turns out you have been carrying it for thirty years.

That is the one we would most like to read.

**OPENING**

Some words are easier to put on a page than others. This one has been sitting on the calendar all year, waiting.

---

Nobody warned you that this one would be different.

The other months asked you about your relationships, your money, your purpose, your work. Hard questions, yes. But they were mostly about things outside you. Things you could point to and name and work on.

August points inward. And that is a different kind of hard.

Mental health is not a weakness. It is not a character flaw. It is not what happens to people who do not have enough faith or enough discipline or enough of whatever it is we think strong people are supposed to have. It is what happens to human beings who are carrying more than they were designed to carry alone, for longer than anyone ever talks about.

Some of you have been managing it quietly for years. Keeping it together on the outside while something heavier runs underneath. Smiling at the right moments. Showing up. Functioning. And telling almost nobody what it actually costs you to do that every single day.

Some of you are in the middle of it right now. Anxiety that will not settle. A sadness that does not have a clear reason. Loneliness that sits in a room full of people. The exhaustion of carrying things that were never yours to carry alone.

And some of you opened this page not sure if it applies to you. You are fine, you think. Mostly. But there is something, some low hum of not-quite-right that you have learned to live with and stopped expecting to change.

This month is for all three of you.

We are not going to fix it in thirty one days. That is not what this is. But we are going to name it. We are going to sit with it honestly. We are going to bring it somewhere real, not to perform healing, but to begin the slow and dignified work of not carrying it alone anymore.

August says: you are allowed to not be okay. And you are not allowed to keep pretending you are.

That is not a small invitation. It is, for some of you, the most important thing anyone has said to you in a very long time.

Welcome to August. We are glad you are here.

---

**CLOSING**

There is a moment in the book of Kings where a man lies down under a tree and tells God he has had enough and would rather not carry on. What arrives is not a sermon. An angel wakes him, feeds him, and lets him sleep. Then feeds him again.

Sometimes that is the whole of it. Food, rest, and somebody who noticed.

Be that somebody for one person this month. And if the person who needs it is you, tell someone. Out loud. This week.

# PAGE 24 · INTERVIEW YOUR OUMA

**PILLAR** · PLAY · **SECTION** · TEN QUESTIONS

**KICKER** · FOR THE WHOLE FAMILY

**HEADLINE** · Ten questions
**for the woman who raised you.**

**STANDFIRST** · Screenshot this page. Sit down next to her. Ask them out loud, and let her finish.

---

Most of what we know about the women in our families we picked up sideways, in bits, usually at a funeral. This is the other way round.

Ten questions. Ask them in order, because the order matters. The early ones are easy and funny and they loosen everybody up. The last two are not, and by the time you get there she will answer them properly.

Children should do the asking. It works better that way. She will tell a grandchild things she has never told her own children, which is one of the odder rules of families and one of the most reliable.

---

## THE TEN

**01** · What did the house smell like on a Sunday when you were my age?

**02** · What was your full name when you were in trouble, and who could shout it loudest?

**03** · What is the naughtiest thing you did that nobody ever found out about?

**04** · Who taught you to cook, and what was the first thing you made on your own?

**05** · What song takes you straight back?

**06** · What did you want to be when you were my age?

**07** · What were you frightened of that turned out completely fine?

**08** · Tell me about an ordinary day you have never forgotten.

**09** · Who is someone in this family I never met, that I should know about?

**10** · What do you know now that you wish somebody had told you at fifteen?

---

## NOW SEND US THE FUNNY ONE

One of those answers is going to make the whole room laugh. Probably number three.

Post it on our Facebook page and tell us which question it answers. Whichever one makes the most of us laugh goes into the September edition, credited by first name and province.

**facebook.com/moxiemag**

Prefer to keep it private? Send the whole conversation to editor@moxiemag.co.za instead. We read every one of them.

**MOXIE TIP**
Do not write the answers down. Put your phone on the table between you, press record on a voice note, and let it run. In thirty years the words will matter, but her voice saying them will matter more, and that is the part nobody thinks to keep.
